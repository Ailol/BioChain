Conflict resolution engages a distinct neurochemical cascade centered on prefrontal-amygdala regulation. The dorsolateral prefrontal cortex exerts top-down control over amygdala reactivity through glutamatergic projections, while the anterior cingulate cortex monitors for error signals — detecting when emotional responses diverge from relationship-preserving goals. Norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus creates heightened arousal that narrows attentional focus, requiring deliberate cognitive reappraisal mediated by ventrolateral prefrontal cortex activation to prevent threat-locked processing. Serotonin from the dorsal raphe plays a critical regulatory role: 5-HT1A receptor activation in the hippocampus facilitates contextual memory retrieval that can reframe current conflict against the backdrop of relationship history, while 5-HT2C receptor signaling modulates impulsivity that would otherwise escalate confrontation. GABAergic inhibition in the central nucleus of the amygdala must overcome noradrenergic drive to enable the calm, measured responses that productive conflict requires.

Hormonally, conflict activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis with cortisol elevation that impairs empathic accuracy when it exceeds optimal levels — the cortisol-to-DHEA ratio determines whether the individual can maintain perspective-taking capacity. Testosterone shows sex-differentiated effects during conflict: moderate levels support assertiveness and boundary maintenance through androgen receptor activation in the medial amygdala, while excessive spikes drive dominance behavior that undermines collaborative resolution. Progesterone and its metabolite allopregnanolone provide anxiolytic counterbalance through GABA-A receptor potentiation, supporting the emotional steadiness needed for difficult conversations. Adrenaline from the adrenal medulla creates the fight-or-flight substrate that must be consciously managed — cardiac coherence and slow breathing directly modulate vagal tone to counteract sympathetic activation. Thyroid hormone fluctuations during chronic conflict contribute to the cognitive sluggishness and emotional fatigue that make repeated unresolved disputes progressively harder to navigate.

The peptide landscape during conflict resolution requires a delicate balance: oxytocin from the paraventricular nucleus facilitates trust and forgiveness through its action on the anterior cingulate and insula, but only when safety signals are present — in threat states, oxytocin paradoxically increases defensive aggression through amygdala potentiation. Vasopressin V1a receptor activation shifts from bonding to territorial vigilance during perceived relationship threats, creating the protective-aggressive stance that must be consciously de-escalated. Substance P amplifies emotional pain through NK1 receptor activation in the anterior cingulate, making perceived rejection feel physically painful and driving defensive withdrawal or counter-attack. Corticotropin-releasing hormone maintains stress-axis activation that sustains the hypervigilant state of unresolved conflict, while neuropeptide Y from the arcuate nucleus provides the stress resilience buffer that determines whether an individual can remain engaged in difficult conversation without emotional flooding. Beta-endorphin release during successful repair creates the relief and renewed bonding that makes working through conflict ultimately relationship-strengthening.
