Dating represents a phase of heightened dopaminergic activity in the mesolimbic pathway, with VTA-to-NAc projections driving reward anticipation and novelty-seeking behavior. Phenylethylamine (PEA) amplifies this through trace amine-associated receptor (TAAR1) activation, creating the characteristic euphoria of new romantic interest. Norepinephrine from the locus coeruleus elevates arousal and focused attention on the romantic target, producing hypervigilance to their communications and presence. Dopamine D2 receptor downregulation in the dorsal striatum creates tolerance effects that drive escalating pursuit behavior, while glutamatergic NMDA receptor potentiation in the hippocampus strengthens encoding of shared experiences into long-term memory with heightened emotional valence.

Hormonally, dating activates the hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis: testosterone rises in both sexes during pursuit phase, increasing confidence and risk-taking via androgen receptor activation in the amygdala. Estrogen facilitates social bonding through enhanced oxytocin receptor expression in the ventromedial hypothalamus. Cortisol patterns show an inverted-U profile — moderate elevation enhancing memory consolidation of romantic encounters while excessive activation triggers approach-avoidance conflict. Dehydroepiandrosterone (DHEA) counterbalances cortisol-mediated anxiety, maintaining the approach motivation despite uncertainty. Thyroid hormone fluctuations contribute to the metabolic acceleration and restless energy characteristic of early romantic pursuit, while progesterone dips facilitate increased sociability and reduced inhibition during courtship encounters.

Peptide signatures include elevated oxytocin from paraventricular nucleus projections during physical proximity and eye contact, vasopressin-mediated pair-bonding initiation particularly active in males through V1a receptor upregulation in the ventral pallidum, and endorphin release through mu-opioid receptor activation during positive social exchanges. Beta-endorphin from the arcuate nucleus creates the warm, pleasurable feelings of early romantic attachment while substance P modulates the emotional intensity of anticipatory excitement. Neuropeptide Y suppression reduces appetite during infatuation phases, and cholecystokinin (CCK) release during shared meals enhances the rewarding properties of dining together. Orexin neurons in the lateral hypothalamus maintain heightened wakefulness and arousal states, explaining the reduced sleep need commonly reported during active dating periods.
