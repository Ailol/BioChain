An established romantic relationship is characterized by a neurochemical transition from dopamine-dominant reward-seeking to oxytocin-vasopressin-mediated pair-bond maintenance. The ventral tegmental area shifts from phasic dopamine bursts associated with novelty to tonic dopamine release patterns that sustain motivation for partner proximity without the frantic pursuit quality of early dating. Serotonergic tone in the dorsal raphe stabilizes, restoring the 5-HT1A autoreceptor sensitivity that was suppressed during infatuation, producing emotional equilibrium and reduced obsessive thinking about the partner. GABAergic inhibition in the amygdala increases through repeated positive partner interactions, creating an anxiolytic effect where the partner's presence activates safety circuitry rather than arousal circuitry. Endocannabinoid signaling through CB1 receptors in the prefrontal cortex facilitates the comfortable, relaxed state of secure attachment.

Hormonal profiles in committed relationships show distinctive patterns: oxytocin receptor density increases in the nucleus accumbens and prefrontal cortex through sustained physical intimacy, creating a self-reinforcing bonding loop. Testosterone moderates to intermediate levels — sufficient for maintaining sexual interest but reduced from the pursuit-phase peaks, reflecting decreased mate competition vigilance. Cortisol reactivity dampens through partner-mediated hypothalamic-pituitary-adrenal axis buffering, where the partner's presence actively suppresses corticotropin-releasing hormone (CRH) from the paraventricular nucleus. Progesterone elevates during nurturing and caregiving interactions, enhancing prosocial behavior through allopregnanolone's positive modulation of GABA-A receptors. Prolactin rises post-intimacy, promoting satisfaction, relaxation, and emotional bonding while creating a refractory period that shifts attention from sexual pursuit to affiliative behavior.

The peptide landscape of established relationships features vasopressin as a dominant bonding signal, with V1a receptor expression in the ventral pallidum correlating with partner preference strength and fidelity motivation. Beta-endorphin release becomes conditioned to partner-specific cues — their voice, scent, and touch activate mu-opioid pathways that produce reliable comfort rather than the intense euphoria of early attraction. Oxytocin pulses synchronize between partners during co-regulation, with paraventricular nucleus activation during conflict resolution facilitating forgiveness and repair. Dynorphin through kappa-opioid receptor activation during separations creates mild dysphoria that motivates reunion-seeking, forming the neurochemical basis of missing a partner. Corticotropin-releasing factor shifts from stress-axis activation to social bonding modulation, and neuropeptide S promotes anxiolysis during shared rest states, reinforcing the security of established partnership.
